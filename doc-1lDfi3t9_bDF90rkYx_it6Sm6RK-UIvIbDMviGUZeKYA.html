Electrolytic capacitors

Construction, charging and discharging | Planned 3 November 2026 | Placement Week 6

Context and scope

Year 13 Physics; teacher: Haaris Jamil. A 25–30 minute learning event, not necessarily a full lesson. Tuesday’s time and room are TBC with my mentor teacher because Year 13 is not shown in the supplied normal Tuesday timetable. Planned teaching only; delivery and outcomes remain blank.

Assessment-standard link

AS 91526: Demonstrate understanding of electrical systems.

Learning intention and success criteria

Relate capacitor construction to capacitance, then interpret charging and discharging observations. Students label electrodes and dielectric, use C = εA/d to explain how effective area and dielectric thickness affect capacitance, explain why electrolytic polarity matters, and describe how capacitor voltage changes during charge and discharge.

Construction and scientific explanation

Use a manufacturer diagram or a purpose-made dry foil-and-insulator model. Do not open or cut an electrolytic capacitor during the event. The conducting electrodes store equal and opposite charges, with electrical energy associated with the field. In an aluminium electrolytic capacitor, a very thin oxide layer is the dielectric; the electrolyte contacts this layer. Etched foil increases effective area, and winding packages a large active area into a small volume. The paper separator is not the thin oxide dielectric represented by d. C = εA/d is an idealised local model: ε = ε₀εᵣ. A thinner dielectric and larger effective area increase capacitance; breakdown strength and construction limit what is practical. Capacitance is not increased merely by charging to a higher voltage.

Safe preparation and go/no-go checks

This plan replaces the proposed destructive demonstration with a safe low-voltage observation. Correct polarity, current-limited charging and resistor discharge are essential. No reverse-polarity connection, deliberate rupture, spark-producing direct short or opening a charged component is included. Reverse voltage can damage the oxide and generate heat and gas; an exploding capacitor is not a necessary learning outcome.

With my mentor teacher, select an intact, modest-energy capacitor and confirm its actual datasheet, rated voltage, polarity, capacitance, working voltage, supply current limit and charging/discharge resistor ratings before teaching. Calculate stored energy E = ½CV² and initial resistor power V²/R to check suitability; choose an RC time constant long enough to observe. Do not choose component values from this generic plan alone. Use a current-limited extra-low-voltage supply with a suitable series charging resistor and a separate discharge resistor or school-approved discharge tool. Connect a voltmeter across the capacitor. Charge and discharge correctly polarised, isolate the source before discharge, and verify a safe residual voltage with the meter before handling. Secure the apparatus, follow school eye-protection procedures and stop for heating, swelling, leakage or odour.

Resources

Capacitor and datasheet, manufacturer construction diagram or dry model, checked low-voltage current-limited supply, suitably rated charging and discharge resistors, voltmeter, leads and school-required protective equipment. If suitable apparatus or approval is unavailable, use a diagram/simulation and defer the live demonstration.

Teaching and learning sequence

Differentiation and evidence

Provide a labelled construction sketch and the meanings/units of C, ε, A and d. Use proportional-change examples before algebra. Extend ready learners through Q = CV, E = ½CV² or qualitative RC reasoning. Retain anonymised labelled sketches, predictions, voltage observations and individual exit responses. Record any construction/model contribution precisely; this plan does not claim the capacitor was built by Haaris.
