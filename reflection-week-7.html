Week 7 Reflection

Week 7: Weekly Reflection

9–13 November 2026

What?

This week, I moved from circuits to electricity generation with my Year 10 classes. In an earlier attempt at the two-lamp challenge, none of the groups in one class finished, even though I gave them about 40 minutes for a task I had planned to take 20. After discussing this with my mentor teacher, I drew the circuit on thick card and colour-coded the connections so students could see how the components in front of them related to the diagram. When I revisited the challenge as a class competition, almost every group finished within 10 to 15 minutes.

At the generation stations, students watched what happened to the voltage reading when they moved a magnet or spun a motor. They offered their own explanations, which gave us a starting point for discussing how electricity is generated in power stations. Later in the week, Science 1 used that idea to design and build turbines. The class completed six models, and students were able to explain how they might improve their designs.

So what?

One of my biggest takeaways was the importance of knowing the students well enough to recognise what is likely to engage them and where they might need support. The card guide gave students a clearer way into the circuit task without making the connections for them. The competition changed how we started, too, so I cannot say that the guide alone made the difference. What mattered was that the students got started more readily and were able to finish a task that had previously stalled.

My mentor teacher had also encouraged me to use a bit more theatricality in my teacher talk. I used the opening to get students curious about what they were about to see, then gave them time to work out why the meter reading changed. My mentor teacher said that the way I used their curiosity was something he would take away from the lesson. That feedback helped me see a useful role for teacher talk with this group: I could use it to get them interested in a question and then give them room to think it through.

Now what?

On the field trip, I want students to test the turbine models they built and use what they see to decide what they would keep or change. The materials limited what they could build, but working within those limits was part of the challenge. I want to hear how they explain their choices after testing, including when a design does not perform as expected. In future practical lessons, I want to keep giving students a clear starting point while leaving the decisions that matter to them.
