Lesson 04: Current and its measurement

Inquiry question Does current change as it travels around a series circuit?

Classes

Y10 Science 2

Date

29 October 2026

Suggested duration

50–60 minutes

Lesson

4 of 12

Unit

Electricity and circuits

Teacher

Haaris

Whakataukī

Mā pango, mā whero, ka oti ai te mahi.

Meaning: Through different contributions, the work will be completed.

Why this lesson: Supports shared practical roles when connecting meters and recording reliable current measurements.

Curriculum alignment

Knowledge

  • Electric current is the rate at which electric charge moves through a conductor, measured in amperes (A).
  • Electric circuits provide a continuous path that allows charge to move through components.

Practices

  • Construct and test series and parallel circuits to observe current flow and circuit function.
  • Measure current and voltage in different parts of a circuit using appropriate instruments.
  • Use appropriate scientific conventions when reporting about electricity.

Learning intention

We are learning to measure current and interpret what current tells us about moving charge.

Success criteria

  • I can connect an ammeter in series.
  • I can record current with a value and unit.
  • I can use measurements to evaluate whether current is used up.

Resources

  • Cells, lamps or resistors, leads and switches
  • Ammeters or multimeters
  • Circuit diagrams
  • Results table

Safety

Use low-voltage supplies. Check meter settings before connection. Never connect an ammeter directly across a cell.

Teaching and learning sequence

Time and phase

Teacher actions

Student actions

Assessment and responsive support

8 min

Refresher and connection

Welcome learners and display the topic. Immediately follow with a 3-minute Lesson 3 refresher: 1 min to sketch and label a working cell–lamp circuit individually; 1 min to compare with a partner and trace the complete path; 1 min to share what happens if one wire breaks and why. Use a student sketch to bridge: “Last lesson we built a working circuit; today we will use an ammeter to measure current at different positions.” Allow 1 min for the whakataukī “Mā pango, mā whero, ka oti ai te mahi”, its meaning and the link to shared practical roles; introduce the learning intention and success criteria. Use the remaining 4 min for individual predictions at positions 1, 2 and 3, with position 3 between cell and lamp. Do not confirm the expected current pattern.

Sketch a working cell–lamp circuit from memory and label the cell, lamp and wires. Compare with a partner, trace the complete path and explain what happens if a connection breaks. Listen to or join in the whakataukī and relate it to shared practical roles. Connect prior circuit building with today’s current measurements, then predict the relative readings individually and explain reasoning.

Use individual sketches, partner explanations and path tracing to check recall before introducing measurement. If needed, offer component symbols and the prompt “What must the path be like for the lamp to light?”; briefly model a complete path if recall is insecure. Ask confident learners to explain the effect of a broken connection. Use subsequent individual predictions to identify support needs without revealing the current pattern; revisit them after measurement.

10 min

Meter modelling

Recall current and amperes; model series ammeter connection, polarity and range selection.

Identify the meter position, setting and safe connection procedure.

Check each learner can identify the series ammeter connection. Give a short guided rehearsal where needed; require independent justification where secure.

15–25 min

Measurement

Assign builder, checker, meter reader and recorder. Check circuits and supervise moving the ammeter with the circuit disconnected. Prompt comparison of positions while keeping components unchanged.

Build and check the loop, read steady current in A and record position/readings. Everyone predicts, discusses and draws the circuit in their own book.

Check safe participation and paired readings. Guide the first reading for groups needing help, then reassess and step back. Offer fault-finding after learners explain their results.

12 min

Reasoning / Plenary

Reasoning and plenary (12 min): use 7 minutes for evidence-based comparison, doubtful readings and consolidation after students explain results. Reserve 5 minutes for the existing individual exit response and a summary linked to the learning intention.

Explain whether measurements support the prediction; recheck doubtful values where feasible. Complete the individual evidence-based exit response.

Check each learner's evidence-based claim. Offer a diagram/sentence starter or brief individual oral response as needed; ask for deeper energy-versus-charge reasoning where secure. Record follow-up needs.

Assessment for learning

Check initial predictions, meter setup, recorded values and units, and whether students revise their thinking using evidence. Use the individual exit response to assess their final claim.

Differentiation: support and challenge

Common learning goal: all learners measure current at three positions in one loop and use their results to explain whether current changes. Vary the support and depth of reasoning while retaining this goal.

Starting point: use each learner's prediction and a brief request to trace the circuit path to identify current understanding. Offer a short teacher check-in to learners who need help identifying a complete path or an ammeter connection; reassess after the first reading.

Access and recording: keep the labelled circuit diagram and the terms current, ammeter, series and ampere visible. Show a simple Position / Current (A) table on the board for learners to copy if needed. Offer the sentence starters 'I predict ... because ...' and 'Our readings ... so ...'. Learners can rehearse an explanation orally or point to the diagram before recording it.

Practical support: use flexible groups and rotate builder, checker, meter reader and recorder roles so each learner contributes. For groups needing support, guide construction one connection at a time and check the first reading together, then step back. Groups ready to work independently check their circuit against the diagram and justify their meter placement before teacher safety approval.

Challenge: after learners have collected readings and explained their pattern, offer the fault-finding task below. Learners needing a prompt trace the path with a finger and locate the ammeter; others independently identify and justify the smallest correction. For deeper challenge, ask learners to draw a new misleading circuit and explain how to correct it using complete-path reasoning.

Plenary and follow-up: retain an individual response from every learner. Offer a labelled diagram and the starter 'My readings show ... because ...' where useful; accept a brief individual oral explanation when writing would prevent a learner showing their science understanding. Ask learners ready for greater challenge to explain why the lamp can transfer energy while current remains similar around the loop. Note which support helped and who needs follow-up.

Provide meter diagrams and fixed ranges. If the practical finishes early, use the 5–10 minute fault-finding challenge before the exit response: individually inspect diagrams A, B and C (2 minutes), agree and justify corrections in groups (3 minutes), then redraw a corrected circuit and explain it (up to 5 minutes). A has an open switch; B has a wire bypassing the ammeter; C is correctly connected. Ask which circuit measures the lamp current, what prevents the others doing so, and the smallest correction needed. Use complete-path reasoning and the rule that the ammeter must be in the path of the current being measured. This is a paper challenge: students should not build the faulty circuits. Teacher notes: close A's switch; remove B's bypass wire; C needs no correction. Do not introduce uncertainty calculations.

Questions to guide discussion

  • Why must the ammeter become part of the path?
  • What pattern appears in the readings?
  • After the investigation: if current is not used up, what changes in the lamp?

Post lesson evaluation

Y10 Science 2: 29 October 2026

The lesson went quite well overall. Most students carried out the experiment and worked productively. However, I did not manage to keep two students on task or get them to complete it. One continued eating despite several reminders to stop, while the other was writing a song in his exercise book. I repeatedly redirected both students towards the activity, but this did not lead to task completion. They did respond to some of my questions, so their participation was limited rather than entirely absent.

After the lesson, I discussed this with my mentor teacher. He provided context about ongoing behaviour concerns and suggested that the fact that our interactions had remained non-abusive was a positive. This helped me put the experience into perspective, although it does not change the fact that I was unable to get these students to complete the learning task. Repeated reminders were not sufficient in this instance. I need to discuss with my mentor teacher a more specific approach for supporting their participation and following through on classroom expectations, while building on their willingness to answer questions.
