Lesson 05: Voltage, current and resistance

Year 10 Science 2 · 2 Nov 2026 · Completed

Full lesson plan

Inquiry question: How does changing voltage affect current?

Classes

Y10 Science 2

Date

2 November 2026

Duration

60 minutes

Lesson

5 of 12

Unit

Electricity and circuits

Teacher

Haaris Jamil

Prior learning and lesson rationale

Prior learning: students have constructed simple circuits, measured current at three positions and discussed charge conservation. The ramp analogy and the link between voltage and electrical energy have been introduced. A short crossword retrieves this knowledge. Students then explore voltage readings and labels on familiar objects before investigating voltage and current through a fixed resistor.

Pre-lesson preparation

Print one crossword sheet and one two-page investigation worksheet per student. Arrange five or six labelled batteries, devices or appliances on the demonstration table. Select intact low-voltage batteries with accessible terminals for measurement and unplugged appliances for label reading. Preselect each label’s relevant input or output voltage. Set the demonstration meter to DC voltage with leads in COM and V. Confirm the resistor rating, safe supply settings and meter ranges with my mentor teacher, pretest the circuit, and write the approved settings on the board.

Whakataukī

Ko te pae tawhiti whāia kia tata, ko te pae tata whakamaua kia tina.

Meaning: Seek distant horizons and hold fast to those you attain.

Why this lesson: Encourages students to build on familiar electrical devices and work step by step towards an evidence-based explanation.

Curriculum alignment

Knowledge

  • Electric current is the rate at which electric charge moves through a conductor, measured in amperes (A).
  • Voltage is the difference in electric potential between two points in a circuit that results in the movement of electric charge, measured in volts (V). Alessandro Volta demonstrated that electricity could be produced continuously to generate current.
  • Resistance is a property of a material or component in a circuit that limits the movement of electric charge, measured in ohms (Ω).
  • Resistors are circuit components that have increased resistance.
  • Energy is transferred between components in a circuit as electric charge moves and interacts with those components.

Practices

  • Measure current and voltage in different parts of a circuit using appropriate instruments.
  • Use appropriate scientific conventions when reporting about electricity.

Learning intention

We are learning to measure voltage and current and use a graph to investigate their relationship.

Success criteria

  • I can connect an ammeter in series and a voltmeter across a resistor, with safe settings.
  • I can record five paired readings with units and plot voltage vertically against current horizontally.
  • I can distinguish voltage from current and use my graph and ratios to support a conclusion, noting the constant-temperature condition.

Resources

  • Per group: adjustable DC supply, one fixed resistor of known resistance and adequate power rating, leads and two meters.
  • Ammeter and voltmeter, or two multimeters; one crossword sheet and one printed two-page investigation worksheet per student, rulers, pencils and calculators.
  • Labelled circuit diagrams in the slides and on worksheet page 1. Worksheet table: Trial 1–5, voltage across resistor (V), current (A).
  • Five or six batteries, devices or appliances with readable voltage labels; teacher-approved low-voltage batteries for measurement and unplugged appliances for label reading.

Teaching and learning sequence

Time and phase

Teacher actions

Student actions

Assessment and responsive support

0-5 min

Do now and retrieval

Display the Do now and provide crossword sheets as students arrive.

Complete the ten-word crossword.

Use responses to notice vocabulary needing clarification.

5-11 min

Everyday voltage and explanation

Invite 5–6 students to read selected labels or measure approved low-voltage batteries; record voltages. Keep appliances unplugged. Give the 2–3 minute ramp explanation: height represents voltage; greater height gives the same ball more energy, while greater voltage transfers more energy for the same charge.

Read or measure the selected voltage, report object and volts, and connect the ramp analogy to electrical energy.

Check the distinction between a labelled rating and a measured battery voltage; ask what greater voltage means for the same charge.

11-20 min

Prediction and circuit modelling

Elicit the worksheet prediction, then introduce the fixed resistor and symbol. Model the series ammeter and voltmeter across the resistor, including DC settings, sockets and polarity. Assign rotating builder, reader and recorder roles.

Commit to a prediction and reason. Identify what each meter measures and trace its connections; prepare to record shared readings individually.

Check meter connections and the prediction before the practical.

20-40 min

Collect paired measurements

20–27: check power-off construction before power is applied. 27–37: supervise five approved voltage settings with the same resistor and circuit, rotating roles. 37–40: check paired readings, investigate doubtful values and direct power-off.

Build from the diagram, record five voltage-current pairs on worksheet page 1, rotate roles after each trial and annotate any corrected reading. Prepare the graph page.

Check that readings belong to the same trial and use consistent units. Confirm meter units agree with the worksheet headings. Note each group's progress.

40-60 min

Graph, interpret and close / Plenary

Graph (40–50): support scales and plotting. Interpretation (50–53): elicit patterns and two non-zero V/I comparisons before introducing R and V = IR. Plenary (53–56): revisit the learning intention and set the existing conclusion and exit check. Pack-up (56–60): direct power-off and collect worksheets.

Plot current horizontally and voltage vertically, use even scales from zero and a best-fit line or curve supported by the points. Compare one point with a partner, describe the pattern and compare it with the prediction. Complete the conclusion and exit check, then pack away.

Check meter placement, what stayed constant and one evidence-supported finding through the existing exit questions. Any unfinished measurements, graphing, calculations, conclusion or exit check carries into Lesson 6. Record the restart point for each group. Pack-up begins at 56 minutes; a brief oral recap can close learning reached so far.

Support and extension

Support and inclusion: keep the circuit diagram, vocabulary and table headings visible. Use plain language, paired rehearsal and targeted questions with thinking time. Support crossword completion with oral clues and crossing letters. Provide help with graph scales where needed. Ensure each student can access equipment and contribute through rotating roles. Check individual understanding through each student’s worksheet and responses. Extension: compare V/I for additional non-zero readings using the same current unit, and consider meter resolution when interpreting differences.

Questions to guide discussion

  • Which two points does your voltmeter connect to, and which component is it measuring across?
  • What does a greater voltage mean for the same amount of charge?
  • What changes and what stays the same during the investigation?
  • What does your graph show? Which readings support that conclusion, and how consistent are your non-zero V/I ratios?

Post lesson evaluation

Y10 Science 2

2 November 2026: Y10 Science 2, Lesson 5 completed

The lesson went all right overall. Quite a few students had missed the previous lesson, so I used the crossword to revisit what they had missed. This took about twenty minutes. I felt it was important to give them a basis for approaching the practical rather than assume that everyone had the same prior understanding.

The practical did not go as well. None of the circuits seemed to work, so students did not obtain the readings needed for the graph. I suspect faulty connecting leads may have contributed, but I have not confirmed the cause. The unsuccessful circuits do not give me a basis for judging whether students understand the relationship between voltage and current.

For the next lesson, I will check the circuit, leads and meters beforehand and repeat the investigation as a teacher demonstration. Students will record the paired voltage and current readings, draw their graphs and use the results to work towards a conclusion. This should let us continue the investigation without spending another lesson troubleshooting several circuits at once.
