Lesson 10: Turbine design challenge

Year 10 Science 1 | Friday 13 November 2026 | 55 minutes

Learning intention

Design, build and improve blades that use wind or flowing water to turn a motor and generate a voltage.

Success criteria

Construct a rotor that turns during a controlled test. Record its movement and meter reading. Change one design feature and use a second test to describe the effect. Explain how the design connects to electricity generation.

Preparation

I will prepare motors with leads attached, stable supports and lightweight homemade centres fitted to the shafts. Card discs will support wind prototypes; plastic centres will support water prototypes. I will pretest one design for each pathway using the actual motors and materials, checking attachment, clearance and a readable meter response. I will prepare card, plastic file covers, popsicle sticks, tape, scissors, pencils, rulers and labelled storage trays. I will provide optional blade outlines and one completed example. I will manage shaft attachments and hot glue, allow the glue to cool and check that it clears the motor bearing. I will set up a protected fan at a marked distance and a teacher-controlled water test over a collection tray. Motors, meters and electrical connections will remain dry. Lightweight card and plastic blades will be checked before testing. Groups will stand clear of the turning blades and stop the test before adjustments.

Teaching sequence

Teacher activity

Student activity

0–10 min: Connect and design. I will introduce “He iti te mokoroa, nāna i kakati te kahikatea” (Although the grub is small, it gnaws through the kahikatea). We will discuss the contrast between the small grub and the large tree and connect perseverance to improving a design. I will allow approximately two minutes for this discussion within the opening ten minutes. Source: https://teara.govt.nz/en/korero-taiao/page-2. I will ask what turned the motor in the previous lesson and what turns generators in wind, hydroelectric and coal-fired power stations. I will show a working blade assembly and the prepared shaft attachment. I will introduce the challenge and invite groups of two or three to choose wind or flowing water. I will check each quick sketch and ask why the material and blade shape suit that pathway.

Discuss the whakataukī and offer an interpretation. Recall the generator investigation. Choose wind or flowing water. Examine the materials, sketch a blade design and identify what will make the motor turn. Show the sketch before collecting materials.

10–28 min: Build. I will distribute motor assemblies and materials. I will circulate, support attachment and balance, and ask students to explain a design choice. Groups needing a supported start will receive a blade outline and construction example. I will check shaft clearance and secure attachments before testing.

Build the first version. Share the making and checking. Use the prepared centre to attach the blades. Check that the rotor turns freely and request a teacher check.

28–38 min: First test. I will supervise wind and water testing. I will keep fan setting and distance, or water flow and position, consistent for repeat tests. I will check meter connections and record the setting. I will ask whether a stationary rotor or an unreadable voltage needs attention to the blades, mounting or measurement.

Take the design to its test station. Observe whether it turns and record the voltage shown, with the meter unit. Briefly note a problem or a feature that works. Use the same station and meter setting for the next test.

38–48 min: Improve and retest. I will ask each group to choose one change, such as blade angle, size, shape or number. I will support one controlled comparison. Early finishers will repeat a test to check consistency and explain how they would adapt the design for outdoor conditions.

Change one feature. Predict what will happen, then test again under the same conditions. Record the reading and decide whether the change helped. Prepare one reason for your conclusion.

48–55 min: Plenary and storage. I will invite two contrasting designs to share one change and its result. I will ask individuals which feature they would retain for the beach test and why. I will connect their findings to the generator and turbine in a power station. I will collect brief responses and organise labelled storage.

Share one result with the class. Individually write which feature you would keep and give one reason. Label the design and store it with its group materials for the field trip.

Prior learning and learner needs

Students investigated a moving magnet and a hand-turned motor in the previous lesson and discussed generators in power stations. Recent lessons showed stronger engagement with practical tasks connected to everyday life. I will use a finished example and prepared shaft attachments to make the starting point clear while allowing different blade designs.

Differentiation

Groups needing support will use a prepared centre, a blade outline and a construction photograph or physical example. I will use short check-ins to help them achieve a first test. Independent groups will choose materials and blade arrangements. Early finishers will change one feature, repeat a test and justify their design choice.

Assessment and recording

Each group will keep a quick sketch and a short record: first test movement and voltage; one change; second test movement and voltage; which version to take outdoors. I will use questions during construction and testing to check the connection between moving air or water, rotation and generated voltage. The plenary includes a brief individual response.

Timing and continuation

I will protect a first test and the closing discussion. If construction takes longer, improvement and retesting will carry forward into the next lesson or the planned field-trip investigation. Completed designs will be labelled and stored for the outdoor test, subject to field-trip arrangements.
