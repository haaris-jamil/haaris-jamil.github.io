Lesson plan

Lesson 09: Turbine design challenge

Year 10 Science 1 | Friday 13 November 2026 | 55 minutes

Learning intention

Design and build turbine blades intended to turn a generator using wind or flowing water.

Success criteria

Sketch and build a turbine rotor, justify one blade choice, and prepare the prototype for a later field test.

Preparation

I will prepare generator kits with leads attached, generator brackets, hubs, wooden dowels, popsicle sticks, screws, wingnuts and turbine bases. I will set out thick paper, cardboard, plastic cups, paper cups, shuttlecocks, plastic containers, compact discs, hot glue and PVA glue. I will pretest one design for each pathway, checking that the rotor is secure, turns freely. I will manage hot glue and check blade attachments before testing. I will check safe use of construction tools and secure blade attachments. Electrical output testing is reserved for the field trip.

Teaching sequence

Teacher activity

Student activity

0–10 min: Connect and design. I will introduce “He iti te mokoroa, nāna i kakati te kahikatea” (Although the grub is small, it gnaws through the kahikatea). We will discuss the contrast between the small grub and the large tree and connect perseverance to improving a design. I will allow approximately two minutes for this discussion within the opening ten minutes. Source: https://teara.govt.nz/en/korero-taiao/page-2. I will ask what turned the motor in the previous lesson and what turns generators in wind, hydroelectric and coal-fired power stations. I will show a working blade assembly and the prepared shaft attachment. I will introduce the challenge and invite groups of two or three to choose wind or flowing water. I will check each quick sketch and ask why the material and blade shape suit that pathway.

Discuss the whakataukī and offer an interpretation. Recall the generator investigation. Choose wind or flowing water. Examine the materials, sketch a blade design and identify what will turn the generator shaft. Show the sketch before collecting materials.

10–28 min: Build. I will show the labelled kit and the two photos of the assembly before blades are attached, then distribute the generator kits and materials. I will circulate, support attachment and balance, and ask students to explain a design choice. Groups needing a supported start will receive a blade outline and construction example. I will check shaft clearance and secure attachments before testing.

Build the first version. Share the making and checking. Use the prepared centre to attach the blades. Check that the rotor turns freely and request a teacher check.

28–38 min: Develop the prototype. I will support groups with blade attachment, spacing and balance. I will prompt students to explain how their design should respond to wind or flowing water.

Continue building. Check clearance with the rotor stationary and explain one design choice.

38–48 min: Refine the design. I will ask groups to review one feature, such as blade angle, size, shape or number, and predict its effect.

Refine one feature if time allows and explain what you predict it will do in the field test.

48–55 min: Plenary and storage. I will invite two contrasting designs to share one design choice and its predicted effect. I will ask individuals which feature they would retain for the beach test and why. I will connect their findings to the generator and turbine in a power station. I will collect brief responses and organise labelled storage.

Share one design choice with the class. Individually write which feature you would keep and give one reason. Label the design and store it with its group materials for the field trip.

Prior learning and learner needs

Students investigated a moving magnet and a hand-turned motor in the previous lesson and discussed generators in power stations. Recent lessons showed stronger engagement with practical tasks connected to everyday life. I will use a finished example and prepared shaft attachments to make the starting point clear while allowing different blade designs.

Differentiation

Groups needing support will use a prepared centre, a blade outline and a construction photograph or physical example. I will use short check-ins to help them complete a prototype. Independent groups will choose materials and blade arrangements. Early finishers will propose and justify one refinement.

Assessment and recording

Each group will keep a quick sketch and justify one design feature. I will ask how moving air or water could turn the rotor. No electrical output was measured in this lesson. The plenary includes a brief individual response.

Timing and continuation

I will protect construction time and the closing discussion. Testing will take place during the field-trip investigation. Completed designs will be labelled and stored for the outdoor test, subject to field-trip arrangements.

Post-lesson reflection

The lesson went well. The students came up with their own turbine designs, and some were quite sophisticated. The available materials limited what they could build, but working within those limits was part of the challenge. Given those constraints, I think they did really well. We ended up with six completed turbine modules, several of good quality. Students were also able to explain how they might improve their designs. We are now ready to take all six modules on the field trip and test them.
