Electrical safety lesson plan

Lesson 11 | Year 10 Science 1 | 18 November 2026 | 55 minutes

Teach this indoor lesson on Wednesday 18 November. The beach turbine investigation is planned for Friday 20 November. Students apply their earlier work on current, voltage and resistance to electrical heating, short circuits and electric shock.

Learning intention

Explain how electrical faults can cause heating or shock and how fuses, MCBs and RCDs respond.

Success criteria

  • Use a circuit diagram to explain why a low-resistance bypass can cause a large current and heating.
  • Use I = V ÷ R to calculate and compare two model currents in milliamps.
  • Identify the protection device for a given fault and say what it detects.

Preparation and safety

Set up a guarded hot-wire station with a school-approved, isolated, current-limited low-voltage supply and the specified resistance wire. Use a heatproof surface, the apparatus’s paper holder or approved long-handled tongs, and small plain paper strips. Provide safety glasses and lab coats. Check the controls, guard, ventilation and shut-off before class. Set and test limits for the exact apparatus in line with school lab procedures. Do not bridge supply terminals with ordinary wire or short any battery.

Students operate the station in groups, with one operator at a time and direct teacher supervision. The operator uses the external controls and holder, keeping hands clear of the wire. Try cutting through a thin paper strip only if the school-approved procedure covers it. Stop at smoke, flame, arcing, damaged insulation or unexpected heating, then isolate the supply and allow the apparatus to cool. If the station cannot be safely supervised or the equipment is unsuitable, have groups analyse an observation or photo of the effect.

Set out battery-powered multimeters, unpowered 10 kΩ and 100 kΩ resistors, calculators and de-energised examples of protection devices if available. Use resistor models by default. Personal readings are optional only with a school-approved, purpose-rated human-contact setup and procedure. Use the model values if that equipment or procedure is unavailable. All 230 V work is on paper.

Lesson sequence

Student activity

Teacher activity and checks

0–5 min | Whakataukī and recall Read and discuss “Mā te huruhuru, ka rere te manu.” Share one thing that helps a group work safely. Recall the difference between current and voltage.

Slide 1. Introduce the translation “With feathers, the bird can fly.” Link preparation and support to safe group work.

5–10 min | Predict the fault Trace the normal route through the lamp and the bypass route. Predict what happens to the lamp and total current.

Slide 2. Give pairs time to predict and explain before taking answers. Use the diagram only. Ask how a pinched cord might create a similar path.

10–25 min | Group hot-wire investigation Assign operator, observer, recorder and reporter. Put on protective clothing. Operate the checked station in turn and use the approved holder to apply the paper strip. Record any visible change and explain where the heat came from.

Slides 3–4. Check PPE and each group before it operates. Supervise one operator at a time at the station. Waiting groups sketch and annotate the circuit. After turns, ask what happened to the paper and relate it to heating in a fault.

25–31 min | Fuse and MCB Compare the devices and explain why each opens the circuit. Classify a pinched cord fault and too many appliances on one circuit.

Slide 5. A fuse link melts. An MCB trips and can be reset once the fault is resolved. Both respond to overcurrent and protect wiring. The hot wire is a heating element with finite resistance and models the heating effect.

31–36 min | Video and discussion Trace a possible path through a person. Identify a factor that might change current through that path.

Slide 6. Play the selected direct YouTube excerpt or use the unplugged diagram prompt. Elicit that a person can complete a path and that moisture and contact can affect resistance.

Assessment during the practical

Ask the reporter to distinguish an observation from an explanation. Look for “The strip changed where it contacted the wire” followed by “The wire heated when current flowed.” If a group only says that the wire got hot, ask what it saw that supports that claim.

Lesson sequence continued

Student activity

Teacher activity and checks

36–46 min | Measure and calculate Check the meter is on Ω with leads in COM and VΩ. Measure the two model resistors, then calculate I for each using 230 V on paper. Use 100 kΩ and 10 kΩ if no meter is available.

Slides 7–8. Meter and resistors stay isolated from all external supplies. Model converting kΩ to Ω and A to mA. At 230 V, 100 kΩ gives 2.3 mA and 10 kΩ gives 23 mA. The model illustrates the inverse relationship and cannot predict a person’s actual shock current.

46–51 min | RCD and comparison Trace current out on active, back on neutral, and along a possible leakage path to earth. Match each scenario to a fuse or MCB, or an RCD.

Slides 9–10. An RCD compares active and neutral currents and trips when the difference is large enough. A balanced active-to-neutral path through a person may leave it untripped. Discuss why a shock can occur at currents well below an MCB’s trip level.

51–55 min | Plenary Answer independently. Why can a short circuit overheat a wire? What happens to current if resistance halves at the same voltage? What does an RCD detect that an MCB does not?

Slide 11. Check for low resistance leading to high current and heating, doubled current, and a difference between outgoing and returning current. Collect the responses before dismissal.

Support and extension

Offer a circuit sketch to trace, a calculator and the conversion scaffold 1 kΩ = 1,000 Ω and 1 A = 1,000 mA. Give students the choice of an observation or recording role at the station. For an extension, ask whether an RCD could detect a shock if all the current returns through neutral.

If time or equipment is limited

Use one group-operated hot-wire station while other groups annotate their circuit sketches. Share the model resistance values if meters are scarce. Keep the final device comparison and individual exit ticket.
