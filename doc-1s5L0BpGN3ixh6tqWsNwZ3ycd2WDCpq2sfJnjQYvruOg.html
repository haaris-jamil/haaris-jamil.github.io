Lesson 06: From voltage and current to resistance

Inquiry question: How does changing voltage affect current, and what can our measurements tell us about resistance?

Adapted after 2 November: Science 2 did not obtain usable circuit readings. Begin by collecting data from a checked teacher demonstration, then graph and interpret it. The original unit plan and Science 1 delivery record remain unchanged.

Whakataukī

Mā te kimi ka kite, mā te kite ka mōhio, mā te mōhio ka mārama.

Meaning: Through seeking comes discovery; through discovery comes knowledge; through knowledge comes understanding.

Why this lesson: Connects scientific investigation with the progression from collecting evidence to recognising patterns and developing understanding.

Curriculum alignment

Knowledge

  • Resistance is a property of a material or component in a circuit that limits the movement of electric charge, measured in ohms (Ω).
  • Resistors are circuit components that have increased resistance.

Practices

  • Measure current and voltage in different parts of a circuit using appropriate instruments.
  • Investigate how resistance changes current using different components (e.g. resistors, wire length).
  • Use appropriate scientific conventions when reporting about electricity.

Learning intention

We are learning to collect voltage and current readings from a teacher demonstration, graph the results and use the evidence to understand resistance.

Success criteria

  • I can record five paired voltage and current readings from the demonstration and plot them on labelled axes.
  • I can identify the series ammeter and the voltmeter across the fixed resistor, and describe the pattern in my graph.
  • I can compare two non-zero V ÷ I ratios using verified units and use the results to explain resistance.

Resources

  • Low-voltage supply or cells
  • Lesson 5 two-page investigation worksheets and graph paper; checked adjustable low-voltage DC supply, fixed resistor of suitable rating, ammeter and voltmeter, and known working leads for one teacher demonstration.
  • Before class, bench-test the complete demonstration at five safe voltage settings. Check each lead, terminal and meter connection and arrange both meter displays so students can read them. Prepare spare tested leads. If a backup data set is needed, label its actual source and collection date.
  • Graph paper or spreadsheet

Safety

Use a current-limited low-voltage DC supply and a fixed resistor of suitable power rating. Confirm safe settings with my mentor teacher and pretest the full circuit. Connect the ammeter in series and the voltmeter across the resistor; check meter sockets, DC modes, polarity and ranges. Power off before rewiring. Keep settings within component ratings and disconnect if a component becomes hot. Any later variable-resistor activity uses a checked lamp circuit at fixed supply voltage.

Teaching and learning sequence

Time and phase

Teacher actions

Student actions

Assessment and responsive support

0–20 min

Brief retrieval and demonstration

0–5: return worksheets and check voltage/current and meter placement with three short questions. 5–20: demonstrate the checked fixed-resistor circuit. Point out both meters, vary supply voltage through five approved settings and pause at each setting for students to read and record the pair. Keep the resistor unchanged and recheck anomalous readings.

Collect the Lesson 5 worksheet. Identify what each meter measures. Record five voltage/current pairs from the same trial on page 1 and check each pair with a partner. Each student keeps an individual record.

Check the first recorded pair before continuing. Provide a table with Trial 1–5 and headings already prepared; read displays aloud as well as making them visible. Ask students who need support to identify one meter at a time. Ready learners note the changed and controlled variables.

20–35 min

Graph the new data

Support graphing the demonstration readings. Model choosing a scale and plotting one of the actual measured pairs, then let students continue. Check one point and the axes before they draw a best-fit line or curve supported by the data.

Use worksheet page 2: current (A) horizontally, voltage (V) vertically; choose equal scales, plot the five pairs and draw an appropriate best fit. Check one point with a partner. Describe the pattern using the data.

Offer prepared axes and a first-point check where needed. Ready learners compare two non-zero V ÷ I values, then test a third. Confirm meter units before calculating; teacher resolves any mismatch with the worksheet heading.

35–50 min

Interpret and connect to resistance

35–45: elicit descriptions of the graphs and compare non-zero V ÷ I values before naming resistance and introducing V = IR. Use plain language about how the quantities change. 45–50, if the class is ready: use a checked lamp/variable-resistor circuit for a brief comparison at fixed supply voltage. Keep the main data and graph discussion as the priority.

Use two measured pairs to compare V ÷ I, discuss what the results support and complete the worksheet conclusion. If the resistance comparison is reached, record current and brightness at two settings and identify what was kept constant.

Support with a calculator, sentence starter and a single pair before comparing ratios. Extend through a third ratio and a prediction checked against a further reading. Ask targeted questions across the room, allowing thinking time. If graphing needs longer, carry the resistance comparison into the next lesson.

50–60 min

Plenary and pack away

50–55: revisit the learning intention. Ask each student to state one finding supported by their graph and identify the current-meter and voltage-meter connections. If resistance has been introduced, include one short V ÷ I example. 55–60: collect worksheets and direct safe pack-up. Record the actual stopping point for the next lesson.

Complete the brief individual exit response: one evidence-supported finding from the graph, and where each meter connects. Submit the worksheet and pack away when instructed.

Check individual responses against the graph rather than relying on whole-class agreement. Note who needs further support and the last completed stage. Unfinished graphs, interpretation or resistance work carry into the next lesson.

Assessment for learning

Individual demonstration data tables, graph accuracy, evidence-supported conclusions, verified units and individual plenary responses. A working teacher circuit alone is not evidence of student understanding.

Support and extension

Differentiation: use the same core investigation with prepared axes, a structured five-trial table, oral and visible meter readings, first-point checks and targeted questions for learners needing support. Extend ready learners through a third V ÷ I ratio and a prediction tested with another reading. Pair checking supports accuracy, while each student records an individual response. Timings total 60 minutes; preserve the plenary and carry unfinished interpretation or the resistance comparison forward.

Questions to guide discussion

  • What must remain constant?
  • What pattern does the data show?
  • What do your voltage/current readings and graph show? Which values support your conclusion?
