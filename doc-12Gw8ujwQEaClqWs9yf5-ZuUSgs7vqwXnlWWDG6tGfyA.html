Observations

My mentor teacher - Restorative meeting

I observed a restorative meeting led by my mentor teacher following an incident involving a Year 11 student. I have left out the details of the incident to protect the student’s identity. my mentor teacher helped the student understand why their actions were unacceptable, made clear the expectations the school had of them, and explained how they could seek support if they were struggling to meet those expectations.

This gave me an opportunity to see restorative practice and Positive Behaviour for Learning (PB4L) in action. What stood out was the combination of accountability and support. my mentor teacher addressed the behaviour directly while making it clear that help was available. For my own practice, this is a useful reminder that maintaining high expectations also involves helping students understand how to meet them.

My mentor teacher - Year 11 whānau time: future pathways

During whānau time, I observed a future-planning session led by my mentor teacher in his role as Dean of Year 11. Each student was asked about their plans for the following year, including whether they intended to return to school, pursue a trade or another training pathway, or move to another school or institution. Students were individually given further information, guidance and counselling relevant to their plans.

What stood out was the individual attention given to students rather than assuming that everyone would follow the same pathway. The session gave me a clearer view of the dean’s pastoral role in helping students consider their options and next steps. It also gave me a better understanding of career pathways for secondary students in New Zealand. For my own practice, it was a reminder to ask about students’ intentions and provide relevant guidance without treating one pathway as the expected choice for everyone. I did not follow the students’ later decisions, so I cannot judge the longer-term effect of the session.

A member of the leadership team - Staff meeting

I attended a staff meeting led by a member of the leadership team, with the Principal present, about an ongoing difficulty involving a student and their whānau. The Principal described the steps the school had taken to support the student with disruptive behaviour and explained that the whānau had interpreted these as targeting the student. The meeting outlined the current situation and the action plan so that staff could respond consistently when working with the student and their whānau.

My understanding of the situation came from the staff discussion. I did not hear the whānau’s perspective directly. The meeting showed me how important it is for staff to share relevant information and agree on an approach. It also made me think about how support can be understood differently by the school and the family, and the need for clear communication.

A Science teacher - Year 9 Science

I observed a large Year 9 Science class continuing a project from the previous lesson. Many students appeared disengaged from the task and were spending time on other activities. a Science teacher remained patient and moved around the room, helping as many students as she could. However, the size of the class made it difficult for her to provide individual support to everyone who needed it.

This made me think about the demands of managing project work in a large class. Individual help is important, but students also need enough structure to continue working while the teacher is supporting someone else. In my own planning, I would consider clear checkpoints and instructions that students can refer to independently. I would need to observe further lessons before drawing conclusions about the class’s usual engagement or the effectiveness of these approaches.

A Science teacher - Year 9 Science

The class was completing an open-book test, and I was asked to support a student who had difficulty with reading and writing. I read the questions aloud, helped the student locate answers in the available material, and wrote down the responses they asked me to record. This gave me a closer view of the support a student may need to access an assessment, and the attention this requires from the adult working alongside them.

After the lesson, a Science teacher said she would like more teacher aides to support students with these needs, but funding was a constraint. The experience helped me understand both the value of this support and the difficulty of providing it alongside the needs of the rest of the class. It also raised a question for my own practice: how do I help a student access a task while keeping clear which parts of the response reflect their own understanding?

My mentor teacher - Year 10 Science

On the last day of term, my mentor teacher took both Year 10 Science groups to the skate park in town to conclude the ramp investigation they had been working on over the previous few weeks. The atmosphere was fairly relaxed. The first group completed the investigation and then had fifteen minutes to buy snacks from the Anchor Inn if they wanted. When the second group went out, rain prevented the investigation from taking place, so my mentor teacher adapted the outing into a field trip around town.

This showed me how a local setting can provide an opportunity to extend an investigation beyond the classroom. It also showed how quickly an outdoor activity can change because of the weather. The two groups had different experiences, and the second group did not complete the planned investigation. For my own planning, I would consider a backup activity that retains the learning purpose, as well as how to provide an equivalent opportunity for a group whose investigation is interrupted.

Assembly - years 7-13

I attended the Years 7–13 assembly on the first day of Term 4. It began with a karakia and the national anthem, followed by kōrero from senior leadership, staff notices and student kōrero. There was also a short quiz, and the assembly concluded with “Whakamau”.

This gave me an opportunity to observe a whole-school routine beyond the classroom. The programme combined cultural practices, notices and contributions from both staff and students. It provided an example of how the school brings different year groups together and shares information as a community.

My mentor teacher - Year 10 Science: adapting the lesson plan

The planned Lesson 3 circuit activity could not proceed because I had been absent on Friday and had not been able to set up the practical workstations. my mentor teacher and I discussed an alternative about half an hour before the lesson and prepared photocopies of a short assessment based on the previous lesson. my mentor teacher led almost the entire lesson. We took the Year 10 class to a small seating area at the edge of the school, where students completed the assessment, then walked through the estuary area and returned to school.

This gave me an opportunity to observe a change of plan close to teaching when the intended practical activity was not ready. The alternative retained a link to previous learning through the assessment, although the planned circuit activity remained outstanding. For my own practice, this highlights the importance of checking practical resources in advance and having an alternative that maintains a clear learning purpose. Reviewing the assessment responses will help identify what students understood and what needs revisiting before the circuit lesson.

The EOTC teacher - Years 7–8 beach EOTC

I accompanied the Year 7 and Year 8 students with the EOTC teacher on an EOTC visit to the beach. Students took measurements to profile the sand dunes and measured what I understood to be seagrass as the starting point for an ongoing investigation of its growth over summer. The plan was to repeat measurements every two weeks until Christmas and use the data to plot graphs.

The students were welcoming. Four groups rotated between activities: two groups took measurements while the other two played with the volleyballs and footballs the EOTC teacher had brought, then they switched. This provided an activity for students while they were not measuring. Along the way, he stopped at the airfield and asked students to work out which direction an aircraft would land from the wind direction. The outing brought together science, physical activity, mathematics through measurement and planned graphing, and group work.

The EOTC teacher was patient while maintaining clear expectations. Before giving instructions, he stopped and asked everyone to look at him. He waited until they did before speaking, even when some students took time to stop their own activities. Overall, this appeared to work well. I also noticed girls and boys grouping separately again; I still do not know why, or what effect this had on participation.

For my own practice, the useful points were planning purposeful rotations, using opportunities along the route to prompt scientific thinking, and securing the group’s attention before giving instructions. The repeated measurements also gave the visit a purpose beyond a single outing. Today established the starting measurements; later data and student graphs would be needed to judge what they learned about change over time.

My mentor teacher - Sea Dogs surfing club

I attended the after-school Sea Dogs surfing club led by my mentor teacher, with three students and one other teacher. It was my first time surfing. my mentor teacher was patient and supportive, explaining the basics and safety. The students were more experienced than I was and readily offered tips and encouragement.

This showed me another side of the school beyond academic lessons. As a beginner, I experienced the support of both the teacher and the students, and saw students taking on the role of helping someone else learn. The session strengthened my impression of a close, supportive school community and showed how relationships can develop through shared activities outside the classroom. For my own practice, it reinforced the value of participating in school life beyond lessons, making it comfortable to ask for help, and giving students opportunities to share their expertise.
