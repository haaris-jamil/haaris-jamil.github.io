Week 8 Reflection

16–20 November 2026 | Placement Week 8

This reflection follows Rolfe et al.’s (2001) “What? So what? Now what?” framework.

What?

The final week combined electrical safety with testing students’ turbines at the beach. Only ten students attended the safety lesson. I felt almost all met the objectives, but safety constraints meant several activities became demonstrations, limiting hands-on participation.

On Thursday, Science 2’s windmills worked well, but the time was not sufficient to build a functioning reservoir for the water turbines. Nevertheless, we were able to test them using a nearby hose. Friday’s lesson with Science 1 went more smoothly, since we had better equipment and were already prepared for the possibility of difficulties. Although the reservoir worked better, it still did not function as a proper dam. We were able to demonstrate how it worked, and also had time for a short farewell message to the students.

All 28 feedback respondents reported receiving help when they struggled. Ratings of challenge and comfort in contributing were more varied. Suggestions included more time, manageable lesson sections and notes for revisiting missed learning.

So what?

The beach lessons helped me recognise that I had expected too much within the time available. Construction, testing, discussion and travel all needed more time than I had allowed. We found alternatives and continued the learning, but could not complete the investigation in the way I had originally intended. My mentor teacher acknowledged the planning, enthusiasm and creativity, and suggested a two-hour session with smaller groups.

The safety lesson made me think about how students could take a more active role even when they could not safely carry out an activity themselves. They could still make predictions and explain what they observed. I felt that most of those present understood the ideas, but I would need individual responses to judge this more confidently. Black and Wiliam (2009) emphasise using these responses to inform what we teach next.

The feedback suggests responsive support was a strength while identifying needs for challenge and accessible notes. Students’ patience during imperfect lessons, alongside the welcome from staff, also made me feel part of the school whānau.

Now what?

For future field investigations, I will allow more time or prepare part of the reservoir, pretest equipment and retain an alternative. I will protect discussion and consider smaller groups. Around demonstrations, I will plan predictions, observation records and individual explanations. I want to retain practical relevance and supportive relationships while checking each learner’s understanding and providing notes for those who miss lessons.

References

Black, P., & Wiliam, D. (2009). Developing the theory of formative assessment. Educational Assessment, Evaluation and Accountability, 21(1), 5–31. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11092-008-9068-5
