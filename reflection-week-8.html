Week 8 Reflection

16–20 November 2026 | Placement Week 8

This reflection follows Rolfe et al.’s (2001) “What? So what? Now what?” framework.

What?

The final week brought together our work on electricity through an electrical-safety lesson and the beach investigations with students’ turbine models. Only ten students attended the electrical-safety lesson. I felt the lesson went well and that almost all students achieved the learning objectives. There were plenty of demonstrations and discussions, but less student activity than I would have liked. Several activities had to be demonstrated because they were not safe for students to carry out themselves.

At Thursday’s beach lesson with Year 10 Science 2, the windmills worked well, but there was insufficient time to build a functioning reservoir for the water turbines. We adapted by using a nearby hose so the turbines could still be tested. My mentor teacher described the field trip as a valuable conclusion to the unit, recognising my planning, enthusiasm and creativity. He also suggested that a two-hour session and smaller groups would provide more time for construction and opportunities for students to share their ideas.

Friday’s lesson with Year 10 Science 1 ran more smoothly because we had learnt from Thursday’s difficulties. We brought better equipment and were prepared for the possibility that the reservoir might not work. It worked better, although it still did not function as a proper dam. We were able to demonstrate how it worked and finish with a short farewell message, wishing the students well for the future.

The combined student feedback also gave me a way to review the placement. All 28 respondents agreed or strongly agreed that they received help when they struggled. Responses about challenge and comfort in contributing were more varied. Written suggestions included allowing time to finish, breaking lessons into manageable parts and providing notes students could revisit after an absence.

So what?

The two beach lessons made the limits of my planning clearer. Building a reservoir, testing turbines, discussing the science and walking to and from the beach placed too many demands on one lesson. Better equipment helped on Friday, but did not remove the time needed for construction. I need to judge an activity’s feasibility by the time and resources students actually have, rather than by how well its idea connects with the topic.

At the same time, an imperfect practical can still support worthwhile learning. On Thursday, using the hose allowed us to continue testing. On Friday, anticipating difficulties helped us respond more calmly and make better use of the available time. I do not want to equate adapting successfully with achieving everything I intended. The demonstration showed something useful, but it did not replace the full opportunity for students to construct, test and improve a working reservoir. My mentor teacher’s suggestion of a longer session and smaller groups gives me a concrete way to improve that opportunity.

The electrical-safety lesson raised a related question about participation. Demonstrating hazardous processes was an appropriate decision, but I could still have given students more responsibility for predicting, observing, discussing and explaining what they saw. My impression that almost all students understood was encouraging, although it was not a separate assessment of each student. With attendance so low, I also cannot assume that the whole class encountered or understood this content.

The student feedback suggests that responsive support was a strength across the placement. It also reminds me that helpful teaching must include sufficient challenge and opportunities for every learner to contribute. Positive comments do not remove the need to respond to the less positive ratings or to students’ requests for time and accessible notes. I need to consider these responses alongside classroom work and my own observations.

The farewell conversations were also important to me. Students had remained patient and willing to learn even when my plans did not work perfectly. Together with the welcome I received from staff, this made me feel part of the school whānau. I leave with greater appreciation of how relationships help a class work through difficulties, while recognising that those relationships need to be supported by clear preparation and realistic expectations.

Black and Wiliam (2009) connect formative assessment with tasks and discussion that reveal students’ understanding and inform the next teaching decision. This strengthens my reason for adding predictions and explanations around demonstrations: these responses could guide my support, as well as increase participation.

Now what?

For a similar field investigation, I will allow a longer session or prepare part of the reservoir beforehand, test the equipment and keep a workable alternative available. I will consider smaller groups and distinguish the essential investigation from additional construction challenges. I will protect time for students to explain what their observations show and what they would change, rather than judge the lesson only by whether the apparatus works.

When safety requires a demonstration, I will plan active tasks around it: an individual prediction, a short observation record, paired reasoning and a brief question to check understanding. I will also make key explanations and notes available for students who have been absent. In future units, I want to retain the practical relevance and responsive support that students valued while giving more deliberate attention to challenge, participation and the evidence of each learner’s understanding.

References

Black, P., & Wiliam, D. (2009). Developing the theory of formative assessment. Educational Assessment, Evaluation and Accountability, 21(1), 5–31. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11092-008-9068-5
