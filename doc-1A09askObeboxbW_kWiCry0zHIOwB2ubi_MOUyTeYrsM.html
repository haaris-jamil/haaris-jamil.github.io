Whakataukī

Mā te kimi ka kite, mā te kite ka mōhio, mā te mōhio ka mārama.

Meaning: By seeking we discover; through discovery we know; through knowledge we understand.

Why this lesson: Introduces the unit’s progression from asking questions and observing effects to developing scientific understanding.

Curriculum alignment

Knowledge

  • Electric charge is a property of matter that causes attraction or repulsion between objects and can accumulate on surfaces (static electricity) or move through materials.
  • Electric current is the rate at which electric charge moves through a conductor, measured in amperes (A).
  • Voltage is the difference in electric potential between two points in a circuit that results in the movement of electric charge, measured in volts (V). Alessandro Volta demonstrated that electricity could be produced continuously to generate current.
  • Resistance is a property of a material or component in a circuit that limits the movement of electric charge, measured in ohms (Ω).
  • Multiple methods are used to generate electricity (e.g. turbines, solar panels). Note: In-depth understanding of electromagnetic induction is not expected at this level.

Practices

  • Use appropriate scientific conventions when reporting about electricity.

Learning intention

We are learning to recognise familiar electrical contexts and identify what we need to understand about electric charge.

Success criteria

  • I can name current, voltage and resistance as electrical quantities, without assuming detailed mastery.
  • I can identify a generation source and an electrical device.
  • I can describe a demonstration and identify a question about charge to investigate in Lesson 2.

Resources

  • Familiar-device/source examples or images
  • Simple circuit-symbol reference
  • Simple atom diagram
  • Approved light-object demonstration materials
  • Charge and static electricity worksheet is assigned to Lesson 2; use oral responses for the introductory baseline.
  • Van de Graaff generator with approved grounding/discharge wand and demonstration materials

Safety

Check for latex sensitivity if balloons are used. Keep charged objects away from sensitive electronic equipment. The Van de Graaff generator is teacher-operated only: follow the school laboratory procedure, check for relevant medical/electronic-device risks, keep it clear of electronics and flammables, and discharge it fully before anyone approaches after use.

Assessment and responsive support

15 min

Electrical quantities

Introduce current, voltage and resistance qualitatively; elicit existing ideas.

Name familiar quantities and identify unfamiliar terms.

Record starting points for Lesson 2.

15 min

Sources and devices

Discuss solar, wind, hydro and familiar devices; connect their energy inputs and outputs. Retain the hand-crank demonstration as prior learning for later lessons.

Contribute examples of sources and devices and describe their uses.

Distinguish generation sources from devices.

15 min

Circuit representations

Show how circuit symbols represent real components and invite questions.

Match symbols to components and ask questions.

Identify symbols needing further practice.

15 min

Charge demonstration / Plenary

Demonstration and plenary (15 min): use approximately 10 minutes for the teacher-operated Van de Graaff and atomic-model explanation under the existing safety procedure. Reserve the final 5 minutes to revisit the learning intention and ask for one observed effect and one question about charge.

Observe the demonstration; describe an effect and contribute a question about charge. Recall the rubbed-rod demonstration when explaining what needs further investigation.

Use oral responses to identify electron-sign and neutral-charge misconceptions for Lesson 2. This revised plenary is a future-use refinement, not a claim about the original delivery.

Assessment for learning

Baseline questions and explanations about quantities, sources, devices and charge; note uncertainties and use them to plan Lesson 2.

Support and extension

Provide familiar contexts, pictures and vocabulary, and accept questions/partial explanations as baseline evidence. Do not rush into circuits or require independent field/discharge explanations. Plan explicit charge teaching in Lesson 2.

Questions to guide discussion

  • What changed when the material was rubbed?
  • Why can a charged object attract something neutral?
  • How could we tell whether two objects carry like or unlike charge?
  • What evidence from the Van de Graaff demonstration showed charge accumulation, an electric field and discharge?

What happened – delivery record

Y10 Science 2 – 12 October 2026: my mentor teacher led the introduction covering current, voltage and resistance, electricity-generation sources, familiar devices and circuit symbols. I then taught for about fifteen minutes, demonstrating the Van de Graaff generator and introducing charging through the atomic model.

Personal reflection - Y10 Science 2

The students showed very little existing knowledge of charges and electricity. The lessons will need to be adapted to accommodate this, and basic fundamental understanding will need to be created before progressing to more advanced applications.

Mentor feedback – after Science 2, before Science 1

After the 12 October lesson, my mentor teacher suggested repeating the introduction with the other Year 10 group, then using my planned charge lesson for their following session. He also suggested a worksheet for students to complete. This feedback informed the repeat delivery on 14 October; it was not received after both lessons.

Revisions and next steps

Lesson 2 now develops the original static-charge learning outcomes: particle charges, neutrality, electron transfer, attraction and repulsion, and explanations of the Van de Graaff demonstrations. Delivery is planned for Science 2 on 15 October and Science 1 on 16 October. The Charge and static electricity worksheet is assigned to the end of Lesson 2. Circuit construction moves to Lesson 3 so that the charge basics are established first.
