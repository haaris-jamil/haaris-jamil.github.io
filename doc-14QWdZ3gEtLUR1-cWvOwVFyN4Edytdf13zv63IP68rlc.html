Lesson 05: Resistance and current

Year 10 Science 1 · 30 Oct 2026 · Completed

Inquiry question How does resistance affect current?

Classes

Y10 Science 1

Date

30 October 2026

Suggested duration

50–60 minutes

Lesson

6 of 12

Unit

Electricity and circuits

Teacher

Haaris

Whakataukī

Mā te kimi ka kite, mā te kite ka mōhio, mā te mōhio ka mārama.

Meaning: Through seeking comes discovery; through discovery comes knowledge; through knowledge comes understanding.

Why this lesson: Connects scientific investigation with the progression from collecting evidence to recognising patterns and developing understanding.

Curriculum alignment

Knowledge

  • Resistance is a property of a material or component in a circuit that limits the movement of electric charge, measured in ohms (Ω).
  • Resistors are circuit components that have increased resistance.

Practices

  • Measure current and voltage in different parts of a circuit using appropriate instruments.
  • Investigate how resistance changes current using different components (e.g. resistors, wire length).
  • Use appropriate scientific conventions when reporting about electricity.

Learning intention

We are learning to interpret our voltage–current graph and extend this understanding to how resistance affects current.

Success criteria

  • I can complete a labelled graph from my Lesson 5 readings and describe the relationship it supports.
  • I can compare voltage and current readings with verified units, then measure current safely at a fixed supply voltage.
  • I can use evidence to explain what happens when resistance changes while supply voltage stays the same.

Resources

  • Low-voltage supply or cells
  • Lesson 5 investigation worksheets, original results and graph paper; a lamp, variable resistor and ammeter or multimeter for each working circuit
  • Connecting leads: check and allocate four working connections per series circuit (supply, lamp, variable resistor and ammeter), allowing for the actual meter probes and terminals. Reuse existing leads and component connections where possible; organise shared circuit stations if complete sets of leads are limited.
  • Graph paper or spreadsheet

Safety

Use a teacher-checked low-voltage supply and suitable lamp/variable-resistor ratings. Begin at high resistance. Check the ammeter is in series and its range is suitable before switching on. Power off before rewiring; adjust the slider by its insulated handle. Disconnect if a component becomes hot. Use one fixed terminal and the slider terminal of the variable resistor, checked against the actual apparatus.

Assessment and responsive support

20 min

Do now: finish Lesson 5 graph

Return the Lesson 5 worksheets as students arrive. Check actual meter units and any negative or unusual readings with groups. Support plotting current horizontally and voltage vertically, following the existing worksheet. Model a scale using separate example data where needed.

Collect the investigation worksheet and complete page 2: label axes and units, choose equal scales, plot the five paired readings and draw the best-fit line or curve supported by the data. Check one point with a partner.

Check axes, scale, units and plotted points. Provide prepared axes or a worked plotting example as needed. Learners ready earlier compare two non-zero V ÷ I values using verified units; flag any readings whose units cannot be established.

10 min

Interpret and connect

Invite learners to describe their own graphs before explaining the relationship. Compare two non-zero V ÷ I values where units are secure. Introduce resistance as V ÷ I and V = IR after discussing their evidence. Explain that proportional voltage and current for the fixed resistor is consistent with Ohm’s law. Then ask how changing resistance at fixed supply voltage might affect current.

Describe the graph and support a conclusion with readings. Complete the Lesson 5 conclusion and ratio comparison. Use the new explanation to make a reasoned prediction about resistance at fixed supply voltage.

Distinguish a straight-line pattern from merely increasing values. Check that learners can separate the previous changed variable, voltage, from the new changed variable, resistance. Supply a clearly labelled comparison data set if original units cannot be verified.

20 min

Extend: resistance and current

Organise a hands-on lamp and variable-resistor investigation in groups. Briefly model safe connections and meter use, then check each group’s series circuit before power is connected. Keep supply voltage fixed and guide groups to collect current and lamp-brightness observations at three to five slider settings. Circulate, use students’ names naturally, listen to explanations and check participation across groups.

Build one series circuit containing the supply, lamp, variable resistor and ammeter. Begin at the checked high-resistance setting. Keep supply voltage unchanged and adjust the slider through three to five settings. Record setting/position, current with the verified meter unit, and lamp brightness in a brief results table. Rotate adjusting, reading and recording roles so everyone handles apparatus. Use the results to explain the relationship.

Provide a simple three-column table: slider setting/position, current (verified meter unit), lamp brightness. Identify which slider direction increases resistance using the actual component. Offer connection prompts and individual help; challenge ready groups to repeat a setting and assess consistency. If leads are limited, allocate complete shared stations and rotate hands-on roles. If graphing needs longer, carry unfinished investigation into the next lesson, retaining student handling of apparatus.

10 min

Plenary and pack away

Allow about five minutes for an explicit individual plenary and five minutes for safe packing. Revisit both relationships: changing voltage for a fixed resistor, and changing resistance at fixed supply voltage. Collect the Lesson 5 graph and the individual response. Carry unfinished graph interpretation or the new practical into the next lesson.

Answer briefly: What relationship does your Lesson 5 graph support? When resistance changes at fixed supply voltage, what happens to current, and what evidence supports your answer? Submit the worksheet and pack away when instructed.

Check each learner’s explanation against their graph and their group’s current/brightness observations. Record remaining misconceptions and the actual stopping point. Retain the existing Lesson 5 worksheet as the opening task; use a short individual response for the plenary.

Assessment for learning

Lesson 5 graph accuracy and evidence-based conclusion; verified units; variable identification in the new comparison; individual plenary response.

Support and extension

Provide graph axes, a scale example and a structured practical table. Check one point individually with learners needing support. Extend ready learners through V ÷ I comparisons and a V = IR prediction. Approximate timings total 60 minutes; preserve graph interpretation and the plenary, and carry unfinished practical work forward.

Questions to guide discussion

  • What must remain constant?
  • What pattern does the data show?
  • What changed in your current readings and lamp brightness as you adjusted the variable resistor? What did you keep the same?

Post lesson evaluation

Y10 Science 1: 30 October 2026

This was a difficult lesson. Six boys had missed the previous lesson and needed to be brought up to date before they could work on the graph. They were distracted and showed limited interest in the task, and I struggled to keep them on track. At my mentor teacher’s suggestion, I seated them together at a central table and set up the previous lesson’s experiment for them. Even with this support, maintaining their attention was difficult. Nevertheless, I worked through the experiment with them before moving on to the graph and the conclusions we could draw from the data.

The time needed for this meant that I had to restructure the lesson as it unfolded. Instead of students completing the planned resistance investigation themselves, I used it as a teacher demonstration. This reduced their opportunity for hands-on investigation, but allowed us to continue developing the connection between voltage, current and resistance within the time available.

I think we still reached the main conceptual learning objectives. From the data, we identified that the ratio of voltage to current was constant for the fixed component, and introduced this constant as resistance. We explored the relationship without using the term ‘proportional’, as students were not familiar with it. We then considered how changing resistance could change current and finished with a few examples. This is my assessment from the lesson discussion; I still need individual responses to judge how securely each student understands these ideas.

The lesson was messy and did not follow the planned sequence closely, but I think I did a reasonable job of managing a difficult situation and keeping the learning moving. It highlighted the need to plan how students who have missed a practical can rejoin the learning without the recap taking over the lesson. I also need to revisit the hands-on resistance activity when there is sufficient time.
