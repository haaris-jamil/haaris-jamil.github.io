Week 1 Reflection

Reflection model: Rolfe et al.’s What? So what? Now what? model

During my first week at Raglan Area School, I observed several Year 10 science lessons taught by my mentor teacher. Other teachers had described this cohort as challenging. In my mentor teacher’s lessons, however, the students generally remained on task and continued working even when the activities were difficult. A visiting student teacher who had seen some of these students in other classes was also surprised by how focused they were.

My first explanation was my mentor teacher’s calm but authoritative presence, supported by his humour and rapport with the students. Closer observation suggested that this was only part of the explanation. His classroom organisation was deliberate: tables were arranged as islands for group work, rows for individual tasks and a circle for class discussions. He also used varied activities and physical models rather than relying on one mode of instruction. The students’ behaviour therefore seemed less like a fortunate response to his personality and more like the result of considered pedagogical decisions.

Even so, I need to be cautious about treating orderly participation as evidence of learning. Fredricks et al. (2004) distinguish between behavioural, emotional and cognitive engagement. I observed persistence and apparent enjoyment, which suggest behavioural and emotional engagement, but I did not collect enough evidence to determine whether students were developing conceptual understanding. Completing a task meticulously does not necessarily mean that the underlying science has been understood.

I had intended to speak with students during breaks, but other commitments made this difficult. I have instead begun initiating brief individual conversations during lessons. In future observations, I will pay closer attention to students’ explanations, predictions and misconceptions. This should allow me to judge engagement through evidence of their thinking, rather than merely through visible compliance.

Reference

Fredricks, J. A., Blumenfeld, P. C., & Paris, A. H. (2004). School engagement: Potential of the concept, state of the evidence. Review of Educational Research, 74(1), 59–109. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543074001059

Rough notes

My mentor teacher supported my placement. The school is beautifully located, between green mountains and the open ocean. my mentor teacher took us for a drive around Raglan, showed us the Ngarunui beach, the wharf and the town centre, which is where the school surfing club happens, which is also led by my mentor teacher. my mentor teacher is a surfing enthusiast. He has a calm demeanour, but a powerful classroom presence. He organizes the classroom seating based on his planned activities. He is also very enthusiastic about building demonstration kits and models. I have been working on building these under his guidance, and we have successfully built several that were already used for learning activities this week. Years 11-13 were writing exams this week, so the lessons were restricted to year 10 only. The other teachers said that they are quite challenging to tackle, but in my mentor teacher’s lessons, they were quite engaged and seemed to enjoy learning. The multiple activities and my mentor teacher’s presence definitely helped, along with his sense of humor. my mentor teacher also drove me to the Raglan recycling centre, where we were looking to find drain pipes to build a demonstration of a musical instrument to model standing waves. I have been busy with model-building and hence haven’t had much time to interact with the students beyond the scheduled lessons. I plan to interact with students during break times to build relationships. I also witnessed a restorative meeting with a student who had broken certain rules - I don’t want to go into details of the offence to protect confidentiality - but I saw PB4L in action, and how my mentor teacher showed care and concern while conveying that this behaviour is not acceptable.

The students were working and trying to complete assigned tasks, even when the tasks were challenging. For one of these lessons, a fellow student teacher also attended, and mentioned that some of the students were disruptive and distracted in other lessons, and he was shocked to see them working so meticulously.

My mentor teacher changed the seating according to the activity. For group work, table islands; for individual work, rows; for class discussions, circles.

I was not able to carry out my plan to interact during breaks because of other commitments, but I have started individual interactions where possible during lessons.
