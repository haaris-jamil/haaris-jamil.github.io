Lesson 01: Foundations of charge and static electricity

Year 10 Science 1 · 16 Oct 2026 · Not delivered

Inquiry question: How can charge explain paper flying off a dome, and what changes when a spark appears?

Whakataukī

Ehara taku toa i te toa takitahi, engari he toa takitini.

Meaning: Success is not the work of one, but the work of many.

Why this lesson: Connects shared explanations and the student atom model with learning through everyone’s contribution.

Curriculum alignment

Knowledge

  • Protons have a positive charge, electrons have a negative charge, and neutrons have no charge.
  • In a neutrally charged atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.
  • Electric charge is a property of matter that causes attraction or repulsion between objects and can accumulate on surfaces (static electricity) or move through materials.
  • Electric fields are regions of space around charged objects where a force acts on other charged objects.

Practices

  • Use appropriate scientific conventions when reporting about electricity.

Learning intention

We are learning to use the charges of electrons and protons to explain static electricity.

Success criteria

  • I can identify electrons as negative, protons as positive and neutrons as uncharged, and explain what neutral means.
  • I can show how gaining or losing electrons changes net charge and explain attraction or repulsion using the like/unlike-charge rule.
  • I can explain paper repulsion using like charge and describe a spark as charge moving during a brief discharge.

Prior knowledge and likely misconceptions

Lesson 1 introduced electrical quantities, sources, devices and symbols, the hand-crank generator, rubbed rod/paper and Van de Graaff. Many students did not know electrons are negative. Revisit the Van de Graaff with paper lift-off and sparks to develop explanation. Do not repeat the rod-and-paper or hand-crank demonstration. Check neutral versus no charged particles, proton versus electron transfer, and magnetic poles versus electric charge.

Teacher and student roles

Teacher: Teach each concept before asking learners to use it. Demonstrate and explain the magnet interaction rather than ask for an uninformed prediction. Revisit the familiar Van de Graaff effects to explain them with newly learned charge ideas. Use questions to check specific understanding; do not assign open-ended discussion or pair work without sufficient knowledge and a substantive task.

Students: Take voluntary roles in the atom model, observe and count charge, then explain changes after they happen. Use the taught like/unlike rule to explain the familiar Van de Graaff effects. Complete the end worksheet independently.

Resources

  • Whiteboard, charge-sign cards and pens
  • Two bar magnets and sealed iron-filings field viewer (or teacher-controlled covered tray)
  • Proton (+), neutron (0) and electron (−) role cards; three distinct colours of hats with written particle labels for the second delivery; simple atom diagram; counters as a seated alternative
  • Charge and static electricity worksheet – begin independently at the END, minutes 50–60. Retain unfinished sheets for the first five minutes of Lesson 3, followed by a brief answer check; no worksheet at the start of Lesson 2 or during demonstrations
  • Van de Graaff generator, small lightweight paper pieces and the correctly earthed discharge sphere/wand; check the actual equipment with my mentor teacher
  • Pepeha section embedded in the Lesson 2 presentation – all 13 slides, including the map and Purkal/electricity context

Pepeha presentation and school-approved recording device/operator

Safety

Teacher operates the Van de Graaff under school/manufacturer procedures; confirm suitable equipment and medical/device precautions with my mentor teacher. Keep learners and electronics clear. Switch off and fully discharge before placing/removing paper; use the proper insulated-handled earthed sphere for sparks. No bodily-contact demonstration or added charge-storage devices. Pre-test paper lift-off and spark effects. Warn before the spark sound; provide an alternative viewing arrangement if needed. Keep filings sealed/covered and away from eyes/electrical apparatus; prevent magnet pinching.

Pepeha preparation and teaching link

The pepeha is the FIRST row of the teaching sequence, with 15 minutes reserved. Record using approved arrangements. Include Purkal village near Dehradun: my first teaching job, learners from surrounding mountain villages, wood cooking and many homes without electricity. This connects my introduction to the unit. All five phases below total 60 minutes, including transitions. Have apparatus prepared; if extra introduction time is needed, shorten the filings display and preserve time for the final check where possible; unfinished worksheet questions will be completed and checked at the start of Lesson 3.

Time and phase

Teacher actions

Student actions

Assessment and responsive support

0–15 min

Pepeha and personal context

Present and record the pepeha and photographs for 15 minutes. Connect Purkal, cooking and electricity access to the unit; invite brief questions.

Listen, view the photographs and ask brief questions.

Notice questions and connections with students' experiences.

15–23 min

Atomic model and charge

Teach proton (+), neutron (0) and electron (−). Guide volunteers with role cards and coloured, labelled hats where available: 3 protons and 3 neutrons in the nucleus, 3 stationary surrounding electrons. Add an electron, reset and remove one; keep the nucleus unchanged. Model a charge explanation.

Represent particles, count positive and negative charges and explain neutral, negative and positive cases.

Check explanations after each change; support with counting and the displayed signs.

23–33 min

Magnets as a visual analogy

Demonstrate attraction and repulsion using magnets and display magnetic field patterns with iron filings. Connect the rule to +/+ and +/− diagrams; explain that poles and magnetic fields are a limited analogy for charges.

Observe, identify attracting and repelling pairs, and distinguish the magnetic analogy from electric charge.

Ask which charge pairs attract or repel and check that students can state a limitation.

33–50 min

Van de Graaff: charge builds, then moves

Repeat paper lift-off using the approved Van de Graaff procedure (about 8 minutes). Prompt pair explanations from the particle model and like-charge rule. Repeat the spark with the earthed sphere (about 5 minutes), teach brief charge transfer through ionised air, and allow about 4 minutes for explanation, transitions and discharge.

Discuss why paper flies off, share an explanation, and identify where charge moves during a spark. Contrast charge accumulation with transfer.

Check that explanations link the paper's acquired charge to repulsion; scaffold from the atom model where needed.

50–60 min

End worksheet and next step / Plenary

Plenary and worksheet (50–60 min): distribute Sections 1–4, restate the learning focus and use Section 4 as the individual exit check. Collect sheets and identify items to finish at the start of Lesson 3.

Complete Sections 1–4 independently, including the paper/spark explanations and exit check. Submit unfinished work as well.

Review charge signs and explanations to decide whether Lesson 3 needs a brief reteach. Carry unfinished items into its five-minute Do now.

Assessment for learning

Check particle signs at the start of the atom activity. After explicitly modelling charge balance, check explanations of the actual added/removed-electron states. After teaching magnetic interactions, check the like/unlike rule. After explaining the repeated Van de Graaff effects, check paper’s same-sign charge/repulsion and charge transfer during a spark. Collect the END worksheet for independent evidence; use my mentor teacher’s observation to refine the second delivery.

Support and extension

Provide role cards, labelled atom diagrams and a seated counter alternative. Keep the same scientific criteria across oral, diagram or written responses. Show a worked explanation before independent questions; reteach if learners cannot use the relevant concept. Optional partner rehearsal follows a taught explanation and a precise prompt only if useful; no compulsory pair work. Extend through model limitations or neutral-paper attraction.

Questions to guide discussion

  • What charge does an electron carry, and what does neutral mean?
  • After changing the student model: how many positive and negative charges remain, and what is the net charge? After demonstrating magnets: how does the like/unlike rule connect to our charge diagrams?
  • Why does paper leave the dome after contact? What moves during a spark, and how is that different from accumulated charge?

Evidence to retain

Baseline oral-response notes, the end-of-lesson worksheet, my mentor teacher’s dated observation, the approved pepeha recording and separate class evaluations. Keep learner evidence anonymised.

Post lesson reflection

  • Which misconceptions appeared most frequently?
  • Could students explain electron transfer and net charge independently?
  • Did the introduction, models and demonstrations form a clear progression? Could learners distinguish magnetic poles, electric charges and charge moving?
  • What should change for the second group, and are learners ready for circuit construction in Lesson 3?
