Lesson 10: Electrical energy and efficiency

Year 10 Science 1 · 13 Nov 2026 · Planned

Inquiry question Where does the electrical energy go?

Classes

Y10 Science 1

Date

13 November 2026 - planned

Suggested duration

50–60 minutes

Lesson

10 of 12

Unit

Electricity and circuits

Teacher

Haaris

Whakataukī

Toitū te whenua, whatungarongaro te tangata.

Meaning: The land endures while people pass away.

Why this lesson: Connects electrical energy use and efficiency with responsibility for the wider environment.

Curriculum alignment

Knowledge

  • Energy is a conserved, quantifiable property of a system that is expressed using a number and a unit (joules) and is calculated from other measurable quantities (e.g. force and distance when calculating work, mass and velocity when calculating kinetic energy) rather than directly measured.
  • Energy is transferred between components in a circuit as electric charge moves and interacts with those components.
  • In circuits, energy transfers are not fully efficient, as some energy is converted to thermal energy and lost out of the system.
  • Energy transfers in circuits can be represented using diagrams that show the quantities of energy transferred.

Practices

  • Use appropriate scientific conventions when reporting about electricity.

Learning intention

We are learning to trace and represent energy transfers in electrical circuits.

Success criteria

  • I can identify the source and destination of energy in a circuit.
  • I can distinguish useful and unwanted energy transfers.
  • I can construct an energy transfer diagram that conserves the total quantity.

Resources

  • Examples or images of lamps, motors, buzzers and heaters
  • Energy transfer diagram templates
  • Optional low-voltage devices

Safety

Do not touch components that have become warm. Use only approved low-voltage devices.

Prior learning and likely misconceptions

Students have used cells, lamps, current and voltage in complete circuits. I will build on that experience to distinguish energy transfer from charge movement, and address the idea that unwanted thermal transfers mean energy has disappeared.

Preparation and organisation

I will select suitable low-voltage devices or clear images, pretest any powered examples and prepare the stated illustrative energy quantities and diagram frames. I will keep hot components out of student handling. Numerical examples will be identified as supplied teaching data rather than measurements from the devices.

Teaching and learning sequence

Time and phase

Teacher actions

Student actions

Assessment and responsive support

8 min

Device observations

I will present approved low-voltage devices or images of a lamp, motor, buzzer and heater. I will ask what each device is intended to do and which outputs students can observe. I will introduce the whakataukī and learning intention, using students' responses to establish the difference between an intended output and an unwanted transfer.

Students will identify each device's intended use and record observable outputs. They will compare a lamp and heater to recognise that a thermal transfer can be useful in one context and unwanted in another.

Check vocabulary for energy transfers.

12 min

Energy accounting

I will trace the energy source, electrical transfer and outputs in a familiar cell-and-lamp circuit. I will distinguish charge moving around a complete path from energy transferred to the lamp and surroundings. After modelling an example, I will ask learners to explain a motor or buzzer example and use their responses to address claims that energy or charge disappears.

Students will annotate a source-to-device-to-surroundings chain and explain where the energy goes. They will identify the role of the circuit in transferring energy and distinguish the moving charge from the energy transferred.

Probe claims that energy or charge disappears.

12 min

Diagram modelling

I will model an energy-transfer diagram using explicitly illustrative data: 100 J transferred into a device, 40 J in the intended output and 60 J transferred thermally to the surroundings. I will label each arrow and show how the output quantities add to the input. These figures are teaching examples rather than measurements of the displayed equipment. I will ask learners to check the total before modelling a second device with a different intended use.

Students will follow the model, label input and output arrows, and check that the quantities balance. They will identify which transfer is useful for the stated purpose and explain why thermal energy has not been destroyed.

Check conservation and useful/unwanted classifications.

18 min

Student application

I will provide two short device cases with stated input and output energy quantities. Students will first complete a partially labelled example, then independently draw a second diagram. I will circulate to check labels, quantities and the device's intended purpose. Ready learners will calculate a missing output from the stated total and compare the useful-energy fraction between two examples.

Students will construct and label energy-transfer diagrams using the supplied illustrative quantities. They will check that output quantities equal input, identify useful and unwanted transfers, and explain the choice using the device's purpose. Each learner will retain an individual diagram.

Check proportional quantities and totals; extend with numerical cases.

10 min

Exit check / Plenary

I will use the first five minutes for an individual exit response: identify the useful and unwanted outputs of one device and explain why inefficiency does not mean energy is destroyed. I will compare selected answers with the learning intention, address one misconception and identify work to revisit. The final five minutes are reserved for collecting work and safe pack-up.

Students will complete the brief exit response independently, check their own diagram totals and submit their work. They will disconnect any practical equipment and pack away when directed.

Use responses to distinguish inefficiency from energy destruction and identify corrections needed next lesson.

Assessment for learning

I will assess students' source-and-output annotations, the quantities and labels in each individual diagram, their classification of transfers in relation to purpose, and their exit explanation. I will use these responses to distinguish insecure arithmetic from a misconception about conservation and record the relevant follow-up.

Support and extension

I will offer labelled diagram frames, energy vocabulary, counters representing equal energy quantities and a worked example where needed. Learners may rehearse their explanation orally before recording it. Ready learners will calculate a missing output, compare useful-energy fractions or explain why the same thermal transfer has a different value in a heater and a lamp. If application takes longer, I will retain the individual conservation check and carry unfinished comparisons forward.

Questions to guide discussion

  • What carries energy through the circuit?
  • Which transfer is useful for the device purpose?
  • If energy is conserved, what does inefficient mean?
