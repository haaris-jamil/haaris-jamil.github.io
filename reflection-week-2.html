Week 2 Reflection

Reflection model: Rolfe et al.’s What? So what? Now what? model

During Week 2, my mentor teacher’s involvement in the school production meant that I took greater responsibility for a Year 11 science class, supported by a relieving teacher. The students were expected to continue a ramp investigation and complete an accompanying worksheet. my mentor teacher had asked me to respond to individual needs rather than enforce discipline. This left me with a familiar scientific task but an unfamiliar class whose relationships and routines I had not yet established.

Initially, only about one-third of the class appeared engaged. I approached individuals and asked whether they needed help rather than directing them to work. This brought several students back to the task, and engagement appeared to increase to roughly half the class. Progress in their written responses provided some evidence that learning continued. However, a substantial group remained disengaged despite repeated offers of assistance. I respected my mentor teacher’s instruction not to assume a disciplinary role, but the experience exposed the limits of subject knowledge on its own. Knowing the physics concepts did not automatically give me the authority, relationships or contextual knowledge needed to teach the class effectively.

Afterwards, my mentor teacher and I reflected on the lesson. He felt that the preceding whole-class discussion had not prepared students sufficiently for independent work. I also recognised that my ability to respond was limited because I knew little about the students, including what interested them and what forms of support they would accept. This is consistent with Roorda et al. (2011), who found that positive teacher-student relationships are associated with student engagement.

My next priority is therefore not to manufacture authority, but to build familiarity and trust through brief individual interactions, learning students’ names and interests, and observing how my mentor teacher re-engages particular learners. Before taking responsibility again, I will also clarify the lesson structure, expected outcomes and boundaries of my role.

Reference

Roorda, D. L., Koomen, H. M. Y., Spilt, J. L., & Oort, F. J. (2011). The influence of affective teacher–student relationships on students’ school engagement and achievement: A meta-analytic approach. Review of Educational Research, 81(4), 493–529. https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654311421793

Rough notes

Monday - met years 11-13, restorative meeting, observed my mentor teacher change seating plan according to activity

Tuesday - Observed the relieving teacher’s lesson, helped a student as reader and writer for test, helped guide the lesson for 20 minutes while my mentor teacher was gone for production rehearsal, with the relieving teacher as the relieving teacher

Wednesday - my mentor teacher just with production, led years 11 and 13 in his absence, but with the help of a relieving teacher. Year 11 continued with their ongoing ramp investigation. Guided year 13 through exam prep and self study.

Thursday - Planned and co-delivered the science club. The students (primary) were given playdough, aluminum foil and popsicle sticks to build boats out of and to work in groups. Led a demonstration for year 12 waves, showing them how a standing wave can be setup on a string or a plate. Year 10 went to the skate park to culminate the ramp investigation.

Friday - last day of term, field trips to town.

Focus area - Taking responsibility for years 11 and 13

  • Year 11 had to complete the ramp investigation they had started earlier. my mentor teacher had asked met o guide them based on individual needs.
  • I had to make decisions about what to do with disengaged learners.
  • The class size was big, with many of them disengaged, and did not see me as much of an authority.
  • A majority of the students responded well. Initially, maybe 30% were engaged. I went up to the ones who were not, and simply asked if I could help them in any way. This brought engagement up to about 50-60%. The rest were still disengaged, despite repeated offers of help. my mentor teacher had explicitly told me not to try and enforce discipline, so I did not go further.
  • The students had a well defined task - an investigation to complete on a worksheet, with an experiment setup where they could come forward to collect data in groups of 2-3. The evidence was the progress in their written responses.
  • The relieving teacher was moving around, talking to the students and trying to get them to stay on task. She was active, polite but largely ineffective.
  • That knowing physics is not the same as teaching it, duh
  • After the lesson, I had updated my mentor teacher, and we performed a joint reflection. He thought that he had not setup the lesson well for me - he had conducted a circle time, discussing concepts from a previous exam, but that circle time was not engaging enough. For my part, I need to know the students and build relationships with them and know about them to know what keeps them engaged.

Year 13 was mostly just self-studying, and was much smaller in size, with 6 students. I helped students when they had questions. Not much more.
